# Fact sheet

Every fact shown on classican, with the subject the painting depicts and where the fact can be
checked. Only facts that are widely documented are used; anything a source calls a legend is
worded as a story.

## Gymnopédie No. 1 · Erik Satie

Painting: dancers in profile, like figures on an ancient Greek vase, moving slowly round a frieze.

1. Satie wrote his three Gymnopédies in 1888, in his early twenties. *(motif: the dancers)*
2. The title comes from the gymnopaedia, a festival in ancient Sparta where young men danced. *(motif: the frieze)*
3. Satie was playing piano in Montmartre's cabarets, such as Le Chat Noir, around the time he wrote them. *(motif: the composer)*
4. The score asks for it to be played "Lent et douloureux": slow and sorrowful.
5. Claude Debussy, Satie's friend, later arranged two of the Gymnopédies for orchestra.

Sources: [Gymnopédies (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnop%C3%A9dies),
[Gymnopaedia (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnopaedia),
[Erik Satie (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erik_Satie)

## Clair de lune · Claude Debussy

Painting: moonlight over a park at night, a fountain and masked figures, the scene of Verlaine's poem.

1. "Clair de lune" means "moonlight". It is the third of four movements in Debussy's *Suite bergamasque*. *(motif: the moon)*
2. The title comes from a poem by Paul Verlaine, which pictures masked dancers and fountains under the moon. *(motif: fountain and masks)*
3. Debussy began the suite around 1890 but did not publish it until 1905, revising it first.
4. It is in D-flat major, and its opening is marked *pp*: very softly.

Sources: [Suite bergamasque (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suite_bergamasque),
[Clair de lune (poem) (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clair_de_lune_(poem))

## Goldberg Variations: Aria · Johann Sebastian Bach

Painting: a two-keyboard harpsichord by candlelight at night.

1. Bach published the Goldberg Variations in 1741: this Aria, thirty variations, then the Aria again. *(motif: the harpsichord)*
2. It was written for a harpsichord with two keyboards, so the hands can cross without colliding. *(motif: the two keyboards)*
3. Every variation is built on the bass line of this Aria, not on its tune.
4. Bach's first biographer told a story that it was played to soothe a count's sleepless nights. Historians doubt it, but the name of the young harpsichordist in the story, Goldberg, stuck. *(motif: the candle)*
5. Every third variation, up to the 27th, is a canon, where one voice imitates another.
6. This recording by Kimiko Ishizaka was crowdfunded and released into the public domain in 2012.

Sources: [Goldberg Variations (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldberg_Variations),
[Open Goldberg Variations (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_Goldberg_Variations)

## Gnossienne No. 1 · Erik Satie

Painting: the red, top-heavy columns of the palace of Knossos on Crete, and a stave with no bar lines.

1. Satie's title may come from "gnosis", knowledge, or from an old word for a dance of Knossos on Crete. *(motif: the columns)*
2. Satie wrote it without bar lines or a time signature. *(motif: the stave)*
3. Besides its tempo mark, "Lent", the score whispers odd advice to the pianist, like "Très luisant" (very shiny) and "Questionnez" (ask questions).
4. It dates from around 1890. Satie may have been inspired by music he heard at the 1889 Paris World's Fair.

Sources: [Gnossiennes (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnossiennes),
[Henle: Gnossiennes](https://www.henle.de/en/Gnossiennes/HN-1073),
[Gnossienne No. 1 score markings (pianio.blog)](https://www.pianio.blog/article-gnossienne-no-1-erik-satie/)

## Nocturne in E-flat major, Op. 9 No. 2 · Frédéric Chopin

Painting: a piano in a dark room by a tall window, the moon and Paris rooftops outside.

1. Chopin wrote it around 1830–31, when he was about twenty, and it was published in 1832. *(motif: the piano)*
2. The nocturne, a dreamy "night piece" for piano, was invented by the Irish composer John Field. Chopin made the form famous. *(motif: the window at night)*
3. Chopin left Poland in 1830 and never went back. From 1831 he made his home in Paris. *(motif: the rooftops)*
4. Each time the melody comes back, Chopin decorates it a little more, like an opera singer ornamenting a tune.
5. It is in 12/8 time: four slow beats, each divided into three.

Sources: [Nocturnes, Op. 9 (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nocturnes,_Op._9_(Chopin)),
[Nocturne (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nocturne),
[Frédéric Chopin (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fr%C3%A9d%C3%A9ric_Chopin)

## Symphony No. 9 "From the New World": Largo · Antonín Dvořák

Painting: a steamship crossing a dusky sea toward a far city on the horizon, the moon rising, with a cor anglais laid across the foreground.

1. Dvořák wrote the New World Symphony in 1893, in New York, where he was running the National Conservatory of Music. *(motif: the ship)*
2. Listen for the cor anglais, a deeper cousin of the oboe, singing the famous slow tune over hushed, muted strings. *(motif: the cor anglais)*
3. It opens with a few soft chords in the winds. One scholar hears them as a musical "Once upon a time".
4. Dvořák called this movement a sketch for an opera on Longfellow's poem The Song of Hiawatha. He never wrote the opera.
5. A pupil of Dvořák's later set the tune to words as "Goin' Home". It is often mistaken for an old spiritual.
6. Neil Armstrong took a tape with the New World Symphony on it to the Moon, on Apollo 11 in 1969. *(motif: the moon)*

Sources: [Symphony No. 9 (Dvořák) (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symphony_No._9_(Dvo%C5%99%C3%A1k)),
[Antonín Dvořák (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ant%C3%B3n%C3%ADn_Dvo%C5%99%C3%A1k)

## Má vlast: Vltava (The Moldau) · Bedřich Smetana

Painting: a river rising from two springs in green hills and widening as it winds down past a castle on a rock.

1. Vltava is the second of six symphonic poems in Smetana's Má vlast, "My Fatherland", each a picture of his native Bohemia.
2. It follows the river Vltava from two small springs, which join into one current and flow on toward Prague. *(motif: the two springs)*
3. Listen for the music swelling from a trickle into the broad, rolling river tune: Smetana's most famous melody. *(motif: the river)*
4. On the way, Smetana wrote, the river passes a farmers' wedding, mermaids dancing by moonlight, and the St John's Rapids. *(motif: the river)*
5. Near the end it flows past Vyšehrad, the high castle of the first Czech kings, and the castle's theme returns. *(motif: the castle)*
6. Smetana wrote it in under three weeks at the end of 1874, just after he had become completely deaf.

Sources: [Má vlast (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%A1_vlast),
[Bedřich Smetana (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bed%C5%99ich_Smetana)

## Peer Gynt: In the Hall of the Mountain King · Edvard Grieg

Painting: a troll king with a gold crown on a stone throne in a torchlit cave, trolls hopping toward him from both sides.

1. Grieg wrote it for Ibsen's play Peer Gynt, at the moment Peer, in a dream-like fantasy, enters the hall of the troll king. *(motif: the troll king)*
2. Its Norwegian title means "In the Dovre Man's Hall": Dovre is a mountain region, and the Dovre man is the troll king. *(motif: the troll king)*
3. Listen to the tune creep in slowly and quietly, low in the cellos, basses and bassoons, then speed up and grow louder until it is frantic. *(motif: the trolls)*
4. In the play the hall is packed with troll courtiers, gnomes and goblins, and there is "a tremendous uproar". *(motif: the trolls)*
5. Grieg joked that he had written something that "reeks of cowpats" and that he could not bear to hear it. *(motif: the torch)*
6. It closes Grieg's Peer Gynt Suite No. 1, published in 1888.

Sources: [In the Hall of the Mountain King (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_the_Hall_of_the_Mountain_King),
[Peer Gynt (Grieg) (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peer_Gynt_(Grieg))

## Peer Gynt: Anitra's Dance · Edvard Grieg

Painting: a dancer in swirling veils inside a striped desert tent by lamplight, a purse of gold coins spilling on a cushion.

1. In Act IV of Ibsen's Peer Gynt, Peer has left Norway far behind and is wandering the coast of Morocco and the desert.
2. Dressed in stolen Bedouin clothes, Peer is hailed as a prophet by a desert tribe. Anitra, the chieftain's daughter, dances for him. *(motif: the dancer)*
3. Peer tries to win her, but she slips away with his money and his rings. *(motif: the purse)*
4. Listen for the lilting three beats: Grieg marked it "Tempo di Mazurka", in the time of a Polish mazurka. *(motif: the dancer)*
5. It is the third of four pieces in Grieg's Peer Gynt Suite No. 1, published in 1888. *(motif: the lamp)*

Sources: [Peer Gynt (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peer_Gynt),
[Peer Gynt (Grieg) (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peer_Gynt_(Grieg)),
[Peer-Gynt-Suite (German Wikipedia)](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peer-Gynt-Suite)

## Tales from the Vienna Woods, Op. 325 · Johann Strauss II

Painting: a zither lying on a garden table at the edge of a sunlit beech wood, a bird singing on a branch above.

1. Strauss wrote it in 1868. Its title recalls the folk music of the people who lived in the Vienna Woods. *(motif: the woods)*
2. It is one of six Strauss waltzes with a starring part for the zither, a folk instrument with many strings. *(motif: the zither)*
3. Listen to the long introduction: a solo violin, then a flute imitating birdsong, before the waltz itself begins. *(motif: the bird)*
4. Its introduction runs to 119 bars, one of the longest Strauss ever wrote for a waltz.
5. If there is no zither, a string quartet plays its part instead. *(motif: the zither)*
6. Strauss wrote more than 500 waltzes, polkas, marches and other dances, as well as operettas.

Sources: [Tales from the Vienna Woods (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tales_from_the_Vienna_Woods),
[Johann Strauss II (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Strauss_II)

## Piano Concerto No. 1 in B-flat minor: Allegro non troppo e molto maestoso · Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky

Painting: a concert grand with its lid raised on a curtained stage, four horns behind it and bursts of light rising from the keyboard.

1. Listen for the opening: the horns call out, then the piano crashes in with huge chords while the orchestra sings the big tune. *(motif: the horns)*
2. That grand opening tune is heard three times, then never returns in the whole concerto. *(motif: the keyboard)*
3. Tchaikovsky played it to his friend, the pianist Nikolai Rubinstein, in 1874. Rubinstein called it worthless and unplayable. *(motif: the piano)*
4. So the first performance went to Hans von Bülow, in Boston in 1875. By one account it was so popular he had to repeat the finale. *(motif: the piano)*
5. Rubinstein later took back his criticism and became one of the concerto's greatest champions.
6. Tchaikovsky revised it twice. The 1888 version is the one usually played today.

Sources: [Piano Concerto No. 1 (Tchaikovsky) (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piano_Concerto_No._1_(Tchaikovsky)),
[Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyotr_Ilyich_Tchaikovsky)

## Piano Concerto No. 2 in C minor: Adagio sostenuto · Sergei Rachmaninoff

Painting: a piano by an open window at dawn, birches and a still lake outside, a flute and a clarinet lying on the piano lid.

1. After his First Symphony's disastrous premiere in 1897, Rachmaninoff fell into a depression and could hardly compose for three years.
2. Daily sessions with the doctor Nikolai Dahl in 1900 helped him recover. He dedicated this concerto to Dahl. *(motif: the window)*
3. Listen for the soft piano figure at the start, then the flute and a long clarinet solo carrying the melody. *(motif: the flute and clarinet)*
4. That opening piano figure comes from a piece he wrote in 1891 for six hands: three players at one piano. *(motif: the piano)*
5. He wrote this slow movement before the first one. It was premiered with the finale in December 1900, before the concerto was finished.
6. Eric Carmen's 1975 pop song "All by Myself" is based on this movement.

Sources: [Piano Concerto No. 2 (Rachmaninoff) (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piano_Concerto_No._2_(Rachmaninoff)),
[Sergei Rachmaninoff (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergei_Rachmaninoff)

## Pictures at an Exhibition: The Great Gate of Kiev · Modest Mussorgsky

Painting: a great stone gate with a round arch and a dome shaped like a warrior's helmet, a bell tower beside it and a crowd passing beneath.

1. Pictures at an Exhibition is a walk through a memorial show of works by Mussorgsky's friend Viktor Hartmann, who died suddenly in 1873.
2. This finale pictures Hartmann's design for city gates at Kiev, crowned by a cupola shaped like an old Slavonic helmet. *(motif: the helmet dome)*
3. Hartmann's gate won a national competition, but plans to build it were cancelled. *(motif: the gate)*
4. Listen for the walking "Promenade" tune from the start of the suite returning in splendour, and for music that rings like great bells. *(motif: the bells)*
5. Mussorgsky wrote the whole suite in three weeks, in June 1874.
6. It was not printed until 1886, five years after his death. Maurice Ravel's 1922 version for orchestra is the one most often played.

Sources: [Pictures at an Exhibition (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pictures_at_an_Exhibition),
[Modest Mussorgsky (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modest_Mussorgsky)

## In the Steppes of Central Asia · Alexander Borodin

Painting: a caravan of laden camels crossing a wide, sunlit steppe, horsemen riding alongside.

1. Borodin called it a "musical tableau": a caravan crossing the desert of Central Asia under the protection of Russian soldiers. *(motif: the caravan)*
2. Listen for two tunes, a Russian song and an ornamented Eastern melody on the cor anglais. By the end they are played together. *(motif: the steppe)*
3. Underneath, plucked strings plod steadily on, like the hooves of horses and camels. *(motif: the horsemen)*
4. Borodin's own note describes the caravan approaching, passing, and slowly vanishing into the immense desert. *(motif: the caravan)*
5. Borodin was a chemist first, a professor who composed in his spare time. He shares credit for discovering the aldol reaction.
6. He wrote it in 1880 for a celebration of Tsar Alexander II's reign that never took place, and dedicated it to Franz Liszt.

Sources: [In the Steppes of Central Asia (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_the_Steppes_of_Central_Asia),
[Alexander Borodin (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Borodin)

## Danse macabre, Op. 40 · Camille Saint-Saëns

Painting: a hooded skeletal Death playing the fiddle in a moonlit graveyard while skeletons dance, a church clock at midnight behind.

1. Listen for the harp at the very start, plucking one note twelve times: the clock striking midnight. *(motif: the clock)*
2. The legend goes that Death appears at midnight on Halloween and plays his fiddle while the dead dance until dawn. *(motif: Death and his fiddle)*
3. The solo violinist tunes the top string down, so Death's first notes form a sour interval once called "the Devil in music". *(motif: Death and his fiddle)*
4. The xylophone imitates the sound of rattling bones. *(motif: the skeletons)*
5. Near the end an oboe crows like a cockerel at dawn, and the skeletons hurry back to their graves. *(motif: the skeletons)*
6. Stokowski's April 1925 recording of it, heard here, was the first orchestral electrical recording made in America.

Sources: [Danse macabre (Saint-Saëns) (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danse_macabre_(Saint-Sa%C3%ABns)),
[Leopold Stokowski (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopold_Stokowski),
[File:PhiladelphiaSymphonyOrchestra-DanseMacabre.ogg (Wikimedia Commons)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:PhiladelphiaSymphonyOrchestra-DanseMacabre.ogg)

## Jesu, Joy of Man’s Desiring · Johann Sebastian Bach

Painting: a choir in a church gallery under organ pipes, a ribbon of notes flowing in threes, and a trumpet on the rail.

1. Bach wrote this chorale for a church cantata first performed in Leipzig on 2 July 1723, his first year as cantor there. *(motif: choir)*
2. Listen for the flowing triplets in the orchestra: the choir's plain hymn tune sits inside them like a stone in a stream. *(motif: triplets)*
3. The hymn tune is not Bach's own. It is by Johann Schop, from 1642; Bach wrapped it in new music.
4. He built the cantata from an Advent cantata he had written in Weimar in 1716.
5. Bach scored it for trumpet, two oboes, strings and continuo alongside the choir. *(motif: trumpet)*
6. The English title is a loose rendering. The German words mean "Jesus remains my joy".
7. Its fame today comes from the pianist Myra Hess, who published a piano transcription in 1926.

Sources: [Jesu, Joy of Man's Desiring (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesu,_Joy_of_Man%27s_Desiring),
[Herz und Mund und Tat und Leben, BWV 147 (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herz_und_Mund_und_Tat_und_Leben,_BWV_147)

## Orchestral Suite No. 2 in B minor: Badinerie · Johann Sebastian Bach

Painting: a baroque flute with notes skipping off it, and a jester's mask for the jesting title.

1. "Badinerie" is French for "jesting": a musical joke, quick and light. *(motif: mask)*
2. It is the last movement of Bach's Orchestral Suite No. 2, a set of dances in the French style.
3. Listen for the solo flute racing through the tune while the strings answer in short, crisp steps. *(motif: flute)*
4. Its speed and difficulty have made it a favourite showpiece for solo flautists. *(motif: notes)*
5. The surviving parts date from Leipzig around 1738–39. Bach wrote out the flute and viola parts himself.
6. Some scholars think the suite first existed a tone lower, with a violin or an oboe as the soloist.

Sources: [Orchestral suites (Bach) (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orchestral_suites_(Bach))

## Harpsichord Suite No. 5 in E major: The Harmonious Blacksmith · George Frideric Handel

Painting: a forge with a hammer falling on the anvil, a glowing hearth, and rain past the open door, from the legend.

1. The tune is the last movement of Handel's Suite No. 5, an air followed by five variations.
2. Listen for the notes getting quicker with each variation, until both hands are racing at the end.
3. In the first variation, one note repeated again and again in the right hand can sound like a hammer on an anvil. *(motif: anvil)*
4. A story tells that Handel sheltered from the rain in a smithy and heard the hammer ringing. No one has ever proved it. *(motif: rain)*
5. The nickname is not Handel's. It was first recorded in the early 1800s, long after his death.
6. Another account says a Bath music seller, once a blacksmith's apprentice, printed it under that name, which was his own nickname. *(motif: hearth)*
7. Handel said he published his suites in 1720 because pirated, incorrect copies were already going around.

Sources: [The Harmonious Blacksmith (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Harmonious_Blacksmith)

## Mandolin Concerto in C major, RV 425 · Antonio Vivaldi

Painting: a bowl-backed mandolin against a sunset over the Venice lagoon, with the campanile of San Marco.

1. Vivaldi wrote this concerto for mandolin, strings and continuo; it is dated to 1725. *(motif: mandolin)*
2. Listen for the mandolin's bright, plucked notes, set against the bowed strings around it. *(motif: mandolin)*
3. Vivaldi taught at the Ospedale della Pietà in Venice, a home for abandoned children whose girls formed a famous orchestra. *(motif: venice)*
4. Some of those girls played the mandolin: Vivaldi wrote mandolin parts for them in his oratorio Juditha triumphans.
5. Its first movement was used in the 1979 film Kramer vs. Kramer.
6. Vivaldi was ordained a priest at 25 and was nicknamed "the Red Priest" for his red hair.

Sources: [Mandolin Concerto (Vivaldi) (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandolin_Concerto_(Vivaldi)),
[Antonio Vivaldi (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonio_Vivaldi)

## The Four Seasons, Spring: Allegro · Antonio Vivaldi

Painting: birds over a spring meadow, a winding stream and a passing thunderstorm, as in Vivaldi's sonnet.

1. Spring is the first of Vivaldi's Four Seasons, four violin concertos published in 1725.
2. Each concerto came with a sonnet, perhaps by Vivaldi himself, describing what the music paints.
3. Listen for the birds: after the opening tune, the violins trill and chirp, as the sonnet's "birds celebrate her return with festive song". *(motif: birds)*
4. Next come murmuring streams, then a short thunderstorm, before the birds take up their song again. *(motif: storm)*
5. Listen for the soft, flowing passage that follows the birds: the streams the sonnet describes. *(motif: stream)*
6. The Four Seasons is one of the earliest examples of program music: music that tells a story.
7. Bach borrowed this movement's opening idea for an aria in one of his cantatas.

Sources: [The Four Seasons (Vivaldi) (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Four_Seasons_(Vivaldi))

## Oboe Concerto in D minor, Op. 9 No. 2: Adagio · Tomaso Albinoni

Painting: an oboe on a table beside a stack of paper (his father was a paper merchant), by a window over the lagoon.

1. This Adagio is the slow middle movement of Albinoni's Oboe Concerto in D minor, published in Amsterdam in 1722. *(motif: oboe)*
2. Listen for the oboe holding long, singing notes over the gently pulsing strings. *(motif: oboe)*
3. Albinoni was the first Italian known to write concertos with the oboe as the soloist.
4. The famous "Albinoni's Adagio in G minor" is a different piece, largely written in the 20th century by Remo Giazotto.
5. Albinoni's father was a wealthy paper merchant, so Albinoni could compose without a church or court job. *(motif: paper)*
6. Bach admired his music and wrote fugues on Albinoni's themes.

Sources: [12 Concerti a cinque (Albinoni) (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/12_Concerti_a_cinque_(Albinoni)),
[Tomaso Albinoni (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomaso_Albinoni)

## Eine kleine Nachtmusik, K. 525: Allegro · Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart

Painting: five string players on a Viennese terrace at night under a lantern, and a rocket climbing like the opening phrase.

1. "Eine kleine Nachtmusik" means "a little night music". Mozart finished it in Vienna on 10 August 1787. *(motif: players)*
2. Listen for the opening: everyone leaps upward together, a figure musicians call a "Mannheim rocket". *(motif: rocket)*
3. Mozart was working on his opera Don Giovanni at the same time.
4. Nobody knows why he wrote it, or whether it was ever played while he lived. *(motif: lantern)*
5. It was written for two violins, viola, cello and double bass, though string orchestras often play it. *(motif: players)*
6. Mozart listed five movements in his catalogue. One of them, a minuet, is now lost.

Sources: [Eine kleine Nachtmusik (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eine_kleine_Nachtmusik)

## The Magic Flute: Overture · Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart

Painting: a stage with three temples under the Queen of the Night's crescent moon, and the golden flute.

1. The Magic Flute was Mozart's last opera, first performed in Vienna on 30 September 1791, two months before he died.
2. Mozart wrote the overture last, after the rest of the opera was finished.
3. Listen for three solemn chords from the brass at the start. Three runs through the story: three ladies, three boys, three temples. *(motif: temples)*
4. After the slow opening, the strings set off on a quick fugue, one voice chasing another.
5. Halfway through, the music halts for three more chords, then the fugue starts up again. *(motif: temples)*
6. In the opera, the magic flute has the power to change sorrow into joy. *(motif: flute)*
7. At the premiere Mozart conducted, and the librettist Emanuel Schikaneder played the bird-catcher Papageno. *(motif: moon)*

Sources: [The Magic Flute (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Magic_Flute)

## Pathétique Sonata: Adagio cantabile · Ludwig van Beethoven

Painting: the ten-year-old Moscheles copying the sonata by lamplight in a library.

1. Listen for the famous singing melody: it returns three times, always in A-flat major, with wanderings in between.
2. Beethoven wrote the sonata in 1798, aged 27, and dedicated it to his friend Prince Karl von Lichnowsky.
3. The title "Grande sonate pathétique" came from the publisher, though Beethoven liked it. *(motif: score)*
4. At ten, the future pianist Ignaz Moscheles could not afford the music, so he copied it out by hand from a library copy. *(motif: copyist)*
5. His teacher warned him against playing such "eccentric" new music. *(motif: shelves)*
6. When the melody returns the last time, the accompaniment grows richer, carried along by triplets.

Sources: [Piano Sonata No. 8 (Beethoven) (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piano_Sonata_No._8_(Beethoven))

## Symphony No. 5 in C minor: Allegro con brio · Ludwig van Beethoven

Painting: a door with a knocker, the four-note rhythm as four bars, and a yellowhammer on a branch.

1. It opens with four notes, short-short-short-long: perhaps the most famous opening in music. *(motif: motif)*
2. Beethoven's secretary claimed Beethoven said of it, "Thus Fate knocks at the door!" Many scholars doubt the story. *(motif: door)*
3. His pupil Carl Czerny told another story: the notes came from a yellowhammer's song, heard in a Vienna park. *(motif: bird)*
4. Listen for the four-note rhythm everywhere: it can be heard in almost every bar of this movement. *(motif: motif)*
5. It premiered on 22 December 1808 in a cold hall, at a concert of Beethoven premieres lasting over four hours.
6. In the Second World War the BBC used its opening rhythm, which matches Morse code for "V", as a victory signal.

Sources: [Symphony No. 5 (Beethoven) (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symphony_No._5_(Beethoven))

## Pastoral Symphony: Allegro ma non troppo · Ludwig van Beethoven

Painting: green hills and fields in the country, a brook, a cottage, and birds overhead.

1. Beethoven gave this first movement a title: "Awakening of cheerful feelings on arrival in the countryside". *(motif: the cottage)*
2. He said the symphony was "more the expression of feeling than painting": a mood of the country, not a picture of it.
3. Listen for very short motifs repeated many times over, building up the orchestra the way patterns repeat in nature. *(motif: the brook)*
4. A later movement has birdsong written into the score: a nightingale on the flute, a quail on the oboe, a cuckoo on two clarinets. *(motif: the birds)*
5. It is the Sixth Symphony, but it was premiered in the same concert as the Fifth, in December 1808.

Sources: [Symphony No. 6 (Beethoven) (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symphony_No._6_(Beethoven))

## Waltz in C-sharp minor, Op. 64 No. 2 · Frédéric Chopin

Painting: a couple turning in a ballroom under a chandelier.

1. Chopin wrote this waltz in 1846–47, two years before he died, and dedicated it to Madame Nathaniel de Rothschild. *(motif: the chandelier)*
2. Listen for three moods taking turns: a sighing opening, a quicker whirl of running notes, and a slower, warmer song in the major key. *(motif: the dancers)*
3. The quick whirling section keeps coming back, like a dance floor you are pulled onto again: the waltz runs A, B, C, B, A, B.
4. Orchestrated, this waltz later became part of the ballet Les Sylphides. *(motif: the dancers)*

Sources: [Waltz in C-sharp minor, Op. 64, No. 2 (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waltz_in_C-sharp_minor,_Op._64,_No._2_(Chopin))

## Ballade No. 1 in G minor, Op. 23 · Frédéric Chopin

Painting: a tower on a cliff above a stormy night sea, a story being told in the dark.

1. The name recalls the old narrative ballad, a minstrel's story-song. Chopin was a pioneer of the ballade as a piece for piano alone, with no words at all. *(motif: the tower)*
2. His friend the poet Adam Mickiewicz is said to have inspired the ballades, though what lies behind each one is not documented.
3. He sketched it in Vienna in 1831 and finished it in Paris in 1835. It was published the next year.
4. When Schumann said it was his favourite, Chopin answered: "I am glad, because I too like it the best, it is my dearest work."
5. Listen for the ending: a thundering chord, then a stormy, headlong coda that finishes with a fiery run of octaves down the keyboard. *(motif: the lightning)*
6. In the 2002 film The Pianist, a performance of this ballade moves a German officer to help the hidden pianist. *(motif: the sea)*

[Ballades (Chopin) (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballades_(Chopin))

## A Midsummer Night's Dream: Wedding March · Felix Mendelssohn

Painting: the wedding in the moonlit wood of the play, heralds' trumpets raised, fairies in the trees.

1. Mendelssohn wrote it for Shakespeare's A Midsummer Night's Dream, played between Acts 4 and 5 for the weddings at the end of the play. *(motif: the bride and groom)*
2. Listen for the opening trumpet fanfare, which tells you a wedding is coming before the march itself begins. *(motif: the trumpets)*
3. King Frederick William IV of Prussia commissioned the music in 1842, sixteen years after Mendelssohn wrote the play's overture at seventeen.
4. In 1858, Queen Victoria's eldest daughter, also named Victoria, walked out to this march at her wedding, and it has been played at weddings ever since. *(motif: the bride and groom)*
5. Much of the play happens in an enchanted wood ruled by the fairy king and queen, Oberon and Titania. *(motif: the fairies)*

Sources: [Wedding March (Mendelssohn) (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wedding_March_(Mendelssohn)),
[A Midsummer Night's Dream (Mendelssohn) (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Midsummer_Night%27s_Dream_(Mendelssohn))

## Italian Symphony: Allegro vivace · Felix Mendelssohn

Painting: the Bay of Naples in sunlight, Vesuvius smoking, a town and cypresses.

1. Mendelssohn began this symphony on a long journey through Italy in 1830–31, inspired by its colour and light. *(motif: the bay)*
2. Listen for the bright, leaping opening: the violins race off over quick repeated chords in the woodwind, as if setting out on a sunny morning. *(motif: Vesuvius)*
3. He finished it in Berlin in March 1833 for the Philharmonic Society of London, and conducted the premiere there himself.
4. He was never quite happy with it, revised it, and left it unpublished. It was printed in 1851, four years after he died. *(motif: the town)*

Sources: [Symphony No. 4 (Mendelssohn) (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symphony_No._4_(Mendelssohn))

## Piano Sonata in B-flat major, D. 960: Andante sostenuto · Franz Schubert

Painting: a lone wanderer on an autumn road at dusk, a bare tree shedding its leaves.

1. Schubert finished this sonata in September 1828. He died two months later, on 19 November, aged 31. *(motif: the setting sun)*
2. Listen for the left hand's gentle rocking rhythm, repeated under a slow, sombre song. *(motif: the wanderer)*
3. It was one of his last three sonatas. He meant to dedicate them to the pianist Hummel, but when they appeared ten years later they were dedicated to Robert Schumann.
4. This slow movement is in C-sharp minor, an unexpectedly distant key from the sonata's home key of B-flat major. *(motif: the tree)*
5. In March 1828, on the anniversary of Beethoven's death, Schubert gave the only public concert of his own music in his life.

## Intermezzo in A major, Op. 118 No. 2 · Johannes Brahms

Painting: an Alpine valley at Bad Ischl on a summer evening, a sealed letter and a rose.

1. Brahms finished his Six Pieces, Op. 118 in the summer of 1893 at Bad Ischl, a spa town in the Austrian Alps. *(motif: the mountains)*
2. He dedicated the set to his lifelong friend, the pianist Clara Schumann. *(motif: the letter)*
3. The tempo marking is "Andante teneramente": at a walking pace, tenderly. *(motif: the rose)*
4. Listen for how the opening phrase keeps returning, turned over and reshaped, as if Brahms were thinking aloud.
5. These late pieces are more inward and reflective than the showy piano music Brahms wrote as a young man.

Sources: [Six Pieces for Piano, Op. 118 (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six_Pieces_for_Piano,_Op._118_(Brahms))

## Scenes from Childhood: Of Foreign Lands and Peoples · Robert Schumann

Painting: a turning globe and a picture book of faraway places.

1. This is the first of thirteen short pieces in Schumann's Scenes from Childhood, the same set as Träumerei. *(motif: the picture book)*
2. Schumann said he wrote them after Clara Wieck told him he sometimes seemed "like a child".
3. He wrote about thirty such pieces and kept thirteen. The titles came afterwards: he called them "delicate hints" for the player. *(motif: the globe)*
4. Listen for the simple tune over gently rippling notes, like a story about faraway places told to a child. *(motif: the ship)*

Sources: [Kinderszenen (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinderszenen)

## Roman Carnival Overture, Op. 9 · Hector Berlioz

Painting: a carnival night in a Roman square, lanterns, fireworks and saltarello dancers.

1. Berlioz built this overture from his opera Benvenuto Cellini, which had only four complete performances in 1838–39.
2. Its music comes from the opera's carnival scene, set in Rome. *(motif: the lanterns)*
3. Listen for the long, singing solo for the cor anglais, a deeper cousin of the oboe, early in the piece.
4. Then the music bursts into a whirling Italian dance, the saltarello, and races to a brilliant finish. *(motif: the dancers)*
5. Berlioz knew Rome: winning the Prix de Rome in 1830 sent him to study there for two years. *(motif: the fireworks)*

Sources: [Le carnaval romain (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_carnaval_romain),
[Hector Berlioz (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hector_Berlioz)

## Carmen: Prelude to Act 1 · Georges Bizet

Painting: the bullring in Seville, a toreador with his cape, a red rose on the sand.

1. Carmen is set in southern Spain, in and around Seville, and ends outside a bullring. *(motif: the bullring)*
2. Listen for the swaggering march of the bullfighters, then the famous Toreador tune, then a dark, slow theme that stands for Carmen and her fate. *(motif: the toreador)*
3. At its premiere in Paris in March 1875, critics attacked the opera's morality and realism.
4. Bizet died three months later, at 36, after the 33rd performance, never knowing how loved it would become. *(motif: the rose)*
5. Tchaikovsky saw it in Paris and called it "one of those rare creations which expresses the efforts of a whole musical epoch".

[Georges Bizet (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Bizet)
